Henri Becquerel Discovers Radioactivity

Overview

In 1896, French physicist Henri Becquerel made a groundbreaking discovery that would change the field of physics forever. While studying phosphorescence in uranium salts, he discovered that these salts emitted a type of invisible radiation without any exposure to sunlight. This phenomenon, later termed "radioactivity," laid the foundation for understanding atomic structure and nuclear energy.

Becquerel initially believed that sunlight was necessary to activate the emission of radiation. However, upon leaving the uranium salts in a dark drawer with photographic plates wrapped in black paper, he observed that the plates still became fogged. This proved that the uranium salts emitted radiation naturally, independent of sunlight.

Impact

This discovery earned Henri Becquerel the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1903, shared with Marie and Pierre Curie, who furthered the study of radioactivity. Becquerel’s work opened the door to revolutionary advancements in medicine, energy, and our understanding of atomic particles.
